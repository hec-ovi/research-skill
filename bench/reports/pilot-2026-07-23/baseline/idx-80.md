## RESEARCH REPORT: Memory Politics, Trauma Theory, and Postcolonialism in Eastern Europe

---

### PART ONE: THE CASE OF BORIS TAKK (As Studied by Ene Kõresaar)

#### 1. Boris Takk's Life Trajectory

Boris Takk was an Estonian whose life spanned one of the most turbulent periods in European history. In 1943, as a teenager, Takk was mobilized by the Germans to join the Waffen-SS during World War II. Rather than retreating with German forces in 1944, Takk made the consequential decision to remain in Estonia, choosing to live under Soviet rule for the following 47 years until the Soviet Union's collapse in 1991. In the early 1970s, during the Soviet period, Takk joined the Communist Party of Estonia—a strategic move that would become central to his post-Soviet narrative. After Estonia regained its independence, Takk recorded his life story, motivated by the death of his mother and meditations on the temporal nature of human life. His autobiography, titled "My Youth in the Turn of History," is now a primary subject of scholarly analysis in memory studies.

#### 2. Activities as a 'Memory Entrepreneur'

Following Estonia's independence in 1991, Takk assumed the roles of witness to history and spokesperson for his generation. As a memory entrepreneur, Takk worked to shape and articulate historical narratives about the Estonian experience during World War II and the Soviet period. His autobiography served as a vehicle for positioning his own life—including his service in the Waffen-SS and subsequent survival under Soviet occupation—as representative of broader Estonian national experience. Through his recorded testimony, Takk engaged in the postcolonial memory work of reframing Estonia's 20th-century history through the lens of national liberation and resistance to foreign occupation.

#### 3. Takk's Core Narrative and His Perspective on "Serving the Estonian Cause from Inside the System"

Takk's narrative centers on what Kõresaar identifies as the concept of "prolonged rupture"—the idea that Soviet rule (1940-1941 and 1944-1991) represented an abnormal, foreign intrusion that fragmented Estonian national continuity. The core of Takk's story frames his actions as part of a "continuous struggle for Estonian freedom," even within the constraints of Soviet institutional structures.

Notably, during the Soviet years, Takk joined the Communist Party of Estonia, which he later justified by claiming he "wanted to serve the Estonian cause from inside the system." This phrase exemplifies a broader post-Soviet pattern in which former Communist Party members in the Baltic states sought to reconcile their Soviet-era institutional roles with nationalist commitments, arguing that they had secretly worked for national interests even while participating in Soviet structures. In Takk's narrative, the Soviet era itself—not merely his individual actions—is presented as a rupture, a colonization that Estonians endured while maintaining internal resistance and commitment to eventual independence.

#### 4. Takk's Stance on the Holocaust

The available scholarly literature on Boris Takk and Ene Kõresaar's analysis does not provide explicit, detailed documentation of Takk's specific stance on the Holocaust. However, his service in the Waffen-SS—an organization implicated in war crimes and genocide—places his narrative within the contested terrain of Estonian memory politics regarding World War II. Kõresaar's analysis of Takk's autobiography is situated within discussions of how Estonian postcolonial narratives frame World War II, suggesting that Takk's account may marginalize or reframe the Holocaust within a narrative of dual colonization. Takk's autobiography, like other Estonian Waffen-SS memoirs, has been subject to scholarly critique regarding how they present revisionist national narratives that can obscure or minimize responsibility for participation in Nazi crimes.

#### 5. Kõresaar's Analysis: The Dominant Postcolonial Perspective

Ene Kõresaar's analysis, presented primarily through her edited volume *Soldiers of Memory: World War II and Its Aftermath in Estonian Post-Soviet Life Stories*, identifies Takk's narrative as exemplifying the "dominant postcolonial perspective" in post-Soviet Estonia. According to Kõresaar, this perspective frames the entire 20th-century history of Estonia through a dual-colonization lens: World War II is understood as colonization by Nazi Germany, and the Soviet period (1940-1941 and 1944-1991) is understood as colonization by the Soviet Union.

This postcolonial framing presents World War II and Soviet occupation as an "abnormal historical period" during which foreign powers destroyed Estonian sovereignty and national life. Within this framework, Estonians are positioned as victims of successive imperial powers and as engaged in continuous resistance toward regaining independence. Kõresaar notes that this narrative—exemplified by Takk's autobiography—became dominant in post-Soviet Estonian memory culture, shaping how official and unofficial narratives presented the nation's recent past.

However, Kõresaar's analytical approach also reveals the tensions and ambiguities within this narrative. By analyzing Takk's story through postcolonialist concepts, Kõresaar demonstrates how national memory narratives are constructed, politically motivated, and selective. The dominance of this particular postcolonial perspective has consequences for how other experiences and voices—including those of Soviet-era Estonians, minorities, and victims of Nazi crimes—are marginalized in public remembrance. Kõresaar's work is thus not merely descriptive; it is critically analytical, examining how memory politics shape competing claims to historical truth and national identity in the aftermath of empire.

### PART TWO: The Perspectives of Svetlana Aleksievich and Jasmina Husanović

#### 1. Svetlana Aleksievich and Her Approach to World War II Memory

Svetlana Aleksievich's approach to World War II memory fundamentally differs from the nationalist postcolonial frameworks prevalent in Eastern European memory politics. Rather than organizing memory around state narratives or national liberation, Aleksievich pioneered what she calls "a history of emotions...a history of the soul"—prioritizing personal experience, affect, and the subjective dimensions of war over official historical accounts.

Between 1978 and 1985, Aleksievich conducted fieldwork for her groundbreaking book *The Unwomanly Face of War: An Oral History of Women in World War II*. She traveled thousands of miles across the Soviet Union, conducting interviews with over 200 Soviet women who had served during the war—pilots, tank drivers, snipers, doctors, nurses, and machine gunners. More than a million women had served in the Soviet military during World War II, yet their experiences were systematically absent from official historical narratives, which celebrated male soldiers and marginalized women's contributions and trauma.

Aleksievich's method involves a polyphonic, oral-history approach that privileges the voices of individuals whose stories were silenced by Soviet propaganda and official commemoration. Her work orchestrates these hundreds of voices into what she describes as a "novel of voices"—a literary-historical hybrid that resists conventional historiography. The Swedish Academy, awarding her the 2015 Nobel Prize in Literature, recognized this innovation as "a new kind of literary genre," praising her work as "a monument to suffering and courage in our time."

The book reveals a profound gap between official Soviet memory (which lionized women soldiers as heroic and unfeminine) and the actual experiences of these women, who returned from war to find that post-war Soviet society demanded they become "carefree girls" rather than women marked by combat trauma. Aleksievich's approach thus exposes how state-sanctioned memory erases the emotional and psychological costs of war, subordinating individual suffering to nationalist narratives. Her oral-history methodology serves as a counternarrative to both Soviet propaganda and revisionist postcolonial frameworks that might subsume women's experiences into larger historical abstractions.

#### 2. Jasmina Husanović's Politics of Witnessing and Hope

Jasmina Husanović, an Associate Professor at the Faculty of Philosophy, University of Tuzla in Bosnia and Herzegovina, develops a framework of "politics of witnessing and hope" that offers a postcolonial and feminist response to trauma in the aftermath of war and imperialism. Her approach intersects with Aleksievich's work in centering marginalized voices and emotional truth, but extends this methodology into an explicitly political and emancipatory direction.

Husanović grounds her work in the specific context of postcolonial, post-war, and post-communist Bosnia and Herzegovina, where the Yugoslav Wars (1991-2001) produced profound trauma, ethnic fragmentation, and ongoing struggles over memory and justice. Her concept of the "politics of witnessing and hope" operates on several levels:

**Radical Relationality**: At the theoretical core of Husanović's work is the concept of "radical relationality"—a belief that it is possible to transcend ethnic and national divisions by finding connections between different traumatic experiences. Rather than allowing trauma to calcify into ethnic or national grievance (as has occurred in much postcolonial and post-war memory politics), Husanović argues for a feminist practice that seeks solidarity across boundaries of difference. This represents a direct challenge to nationalist frameworks that, like the postcolonial Estonian perspective analyzed by Kõresaar, organize trauma around ethnic or national identity.

**Gender and Feminist Lenses**: In her 2009 publication "The Politics of Gender, Witnessing, Postcoloniality and Trauma: Bosnian Feminist Trajectories," Husanović argues that feminist theory and praxis must engage with postcolonial trauma and biopolitics. She emphasizes that trauma narratives are gendered—that women's experiences of war, occupation, and postcolonial violence are systematically different from and marginalized within male-centered nationalist narratives. This aligns with Aleksievich's work on Soviet women in World War II.

**Creative Approach to Loss and Hope**: Husanović's framework involves a "creative approach to losses" and an engagement with "potential politics of hope." Rather than accepting trauma as defining or unchangeable, she argues for political and cultural practices that transform suffering into solidarity, creativity, and emancipatory politics. Her work examines how public spaces of cultural and knowledge production—including art, testimony, and feminist organizing—can generate possibilities for transcending the boundaries imposed by trauma and postcolonial structures.

**Transnational Feminist Solidarities**: Husanović's scholarship bridges "local" and "global" feminist and postcolonial theory, emphasizing that lessons from Bosnian experiences of postcoloniality, post-war recovery, and post-communism can inform broader transnational feminist praxis. Her activist engagement with trauma narratives demonstrates that political change is possible when witnessing is understood not as individual testimony alone, but as a collective, relational, and politically intentional practice.

In essence, Husanović's "politics of witnessing and hope" offers a counterpoint to both nationalist memory politics (exemplified by the postcolonial Estonian framework) and to trauma studies approaches that risk aestheticizing suffering. Her work insists that witnessing to trauma can be transformative—creating possibilities for solidarity, equality, and emancipatory politics across the fractures of postcoloniality, ethnic division, and gendered violence.

---

### Synthesis: Three Perspectives on Eastern European Memory Politics

These three scholars—Ene Kõresaar, Svetlana Aleksievich, and Jasmina Husanović—represent different approaches to understanding how trauma, memory, and postcolonialism intersect in Eastern Europe. Kõresaar's analytical work reveals how postcolonial nationalism organizes memory around state narratives and ethnic identity, using the case of Boris Takk to show how individual life stories become vehicles for dominant political frameworks. Aleksievich's oral-history methodology prioritizes the emotional and subjective dimensions of war, giving voice to those—particularly women—whose experiences are marginalized by official narratives. Husanović synthesizes feminist and postcolonial theory with activist practice, arguing for a politics of witnessing that transcends ethnic and national boundaries in pursuit of solidarity, equality, and hope. Together, their work demonstrates that memory politics in Eastern Europe cannot be understood through any single lens—that understanding requires attention to how nationalism, gender, emotion, and transnational solidarity shape competing claims to historical truth.

## Summary

This research examines memory politics, trauma theory, and postcolonialism through three scholars: Ene Kõresaar's analysis of Boris Takk's postcolonial narrative in Estonia; Svetlana Aleksievich's oral history methodology centered on women's emotional testimony from World War II; and Jasmina Husanović's politics of witnessing and hope that opposes hierarchies of suffering through radical relationality. Takk, a Waffen-SS volunteer, constructed a "continuous struggle for Estonian freedom" narrative framing Soviet occupation as prolonged colonization, which Kõresaar identifies as dominant postcolonial memory in Estonia. Aleksievich pioneered polyphonic writing that transforms individual emotions into collective history, while Husanović develops feminist decolonization strategies emphasizing solidarity across different traumatic experiences.

## Findings

**Boris Takk's Life Trajectory and Activities as Memory Entrepreneur**

Boris Takk was an Estonian figure whose life story has been studied by historian and memory scholar Ene Kõresaar. According to Kõresaar's analysis in "Soldiers of Memory," Takk volunteered to serve in the German Army during World War II, specifically in the Waffen-SS. After Estonia regained independence in the early 1990s, Takk became active as a "memory entrepreneur"—an individual interested in disseminating and fighting for his version of historical truth. He recorded his life story and assumed the roles of a witness to history and spokesperson for his generation.

**Takk's Core Narrative Framework**

Takk's narrative centers on the concept of a "continuous struggle for Estonian freedom." Kõresaar found that Takk framed the entire twentieth-century Estonian experience within a postcolonialist framework, presenting World War II and Soviet occupation as an abnormal historical period representing "prolonged rupture"—a break in Estonian sovereignty caused by foreign powers that destroyed the Estonian way of life. Takk argued for a "third way" concept, positioning Estonians as seeking to survive between two occupying forces—Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union—with the ultimate goal of regaining national independence.

Regarding his service "from inside the system," Takk justified his later decision to join the Communist Party of Estonia in the early 1970s as an attempt to "serve the Estonian cause from inside the system," mirroring his earlier justification for joining the German Army. This strategy allowed him to construct a continuous narrative of nationalist resistance regardless of which occupying power controlled the territory.

**Takk's Stance on the Holocaust**

Kõresaar's analysis reveals that Takk dealt with the problem of guilt—having volunteered for a German military force responsible for atrocities—by attributing responsibility to the colonizing powers rather than to individual Estonian collaborators. This framework made acknowledgment of complicity in Holocaust crimes and acceptance of collective guilt "next to impossible," as researchers have noted. Estonian and Latvian Waffen-SS memoirs in general produced similar narratives justifying Nazi collaboration as having been in the national interest, a pattern that supports arguments that contemporary Estonian and Latvian collective memory rests on revisionist narratives of World War II and the Holocaust.

**Kõresaar's Analysis and the Postcolonial Perspective**

Ene Kõresaar, a senior researcher at the University of Tartu specializing in memory culture studies, analyzed Takk's autobiographical narrative using postcolonialist theory. She found that Takk's life story represents the dominant view of twentieth-century Estonian history evaluated from a postcolonial perspective. This perspective frames World War II as a dual colonization—simultaneous occupation and destruction by Nazi Germany followed by Soviet occupation from 1944 to 1991.

Kõresaar's work demonstrates that the postcolonial lens dominant in post-Soviet Estonian collective memory creates a framework in which both Nazi and Soviet occupations are framed as foreign invasions that interrupted normal national development. Within this framework, Estonians are positioned as victims and resisters of colonization, a narrative that complicates straightforward historical accountability for collaborationist actions.

**Svetlana Aleksievich's Approach to World War II Memory**

Aleksievich, a Belarusian author who won the 2015 Nobel Prize in Literature "for her polyphonic writings, a monument to suffering and courage in our time," pioneered a distinctive methodological approach to World War II memory. Over approximately 26 years beginning in the late 1970s, she conducted hundreds of interviews with female veterans of World War II. Her approach emphasized intimate, one-on-one conversations designed to access deeper emotional truths than formal interviews typically yield.

Aleksievich created what she terms a "history of emotions"—a carefully composed collage of human voices presented as extended monologues. Rather than writing conventional history or journalism, she stylizes oral testimonies according to literary conventions, specifically employing a requiem form in "War's Unwomanly Face." Her methodology involves allowing interviewees to speak without authorial interruption, a practice she explains: "I listen when they speak. I listen when they are silent. Both words and silence are the text for me."

**Content and Scope of "War's Unwomanly Face"**

The work documents approximately one million Soviet women who served in World War II as pilots, snipers, nurses, doctors, tank drivers, machine-gunners, and submachine gunners. The Soviet Union was the first nation to allow women pilots to fly combat missions. Despite surviving combat and death, these women found themselves denied postwar recognition, often denounced as unladylike and unmarriageable. Many maintained silence about their service for decades until Aleksievich's interviews gave them voice.

Aleksievich organized testimonies thematically, exploring time, memory, identity, womanhood, and patriotism. A distinctive finding emerged: women consistently returned to concerns about beauty and femininity even while discussing combat and death, revealing how they maintained their sense of identity within military life. The book presents an alternative history from the personal stories of individuals whose experiences diverged significantly from official Soviet propaganda narratives.

**Jasmina Husanović's Politics of Witnessing and Hope**

Jasmina Husanović is a Full Professor of Cultural Studies and Gender Studies at the University of Tuzla, Bosnia and Herzegovina. Her 2009 article "The Politics of Gender, Witnessing, Postcoloniality and Trauma: Bosnian Feminist Trajectories" articulates her foundational framework for addressing trauma in postcolonial contexts. She examines the politics of witnessing through what she calls a "creative approach to losses and the potential politics of hope."

Husanović's approach departs from both liberal and nationalist framings of trauma memory. She rejects what she terms liberal management of memory "with white gloves" through transitional justice mechanisms like trials and truth commissions, and she opposes nationalist politicians claiming exclusive ownership of traumatic pasts for political gain. Instead, she advocates for civil society spaces where art and activism facilitate healing.

**Radical Relationality and Feminist Solidarity**

Central to Husanović's framework is the concept of "radical relationality"—a belief that it is possible to transcend ethnic and national divisions and find connections between different traumatic experiences. She emphasizes making "real women's bodies" visible in order to create this radical relationality. Her "politics of witnessing and hope" aims to restore dignity to bodies that have been "defaced, erased and/or violently made non-relevant" within power structures.

Husanović engages with dialogue between local and global feminist and postcolonial theory through subaltern lenses of transnational feminist solidarities. She emphasizes vernacular cosmopolitanism in cultural production—non-institutional public spaces of thinking, judging and acting—as sites where emancipatory politics can emerge. Her work, including the monograph "Culture, Community and Activism in Bosnia and Herzegovina: Emancipatory Trajectories" (2020), explores how social movements and civil society groups forge public languages of grief and hope while engaging in politics of social justice.

## Insights

- Kõresaar's postcolonial framework reveals how memory entrepreneurship in post-Soviet Estonia constructs nationalist narratives that recast collaboration with Nazi occupiers as resistance to communist colonization, illustrating how geopolitical positioning shapes historical accountability for atrocities rather than moral judgment of actions.

- Aleksievich's polyphonic oral history method and Husanović's radical relationality both operate as counter-memory practices: Aleksievich collects suppressed individual voices to challenge official Soviet narratives about gender and war, while Husanović seeks to transcend ethnic/national hierarchies of trauma to build solidarity across victim groups—both rejecting monolithic historical accounts.

- The three scholars demonstrate a trajectory from documenting how dominant narratives erase or reframe suffering (Aleksievich on Soviet women, Kõresaar on collaborationist narratives, Husanović on internationalized trauma management) toward methodologies that create space for marginalized perspectives—oral monologue, postcolonial analysis, and emancipatory activism respectively.

- A tension exists between Kõresaar's analysis of how postcolonial framing enables narrative escape from complicity and Husanović's feminist hope-politics: the former critiques how victimization narratives preclude accountability, while the latter seeks solidarity that transcends victimhood hierarchies, suggesting that naming trauma's political instrumentalization is necessary but insufficient without alternative frameworks for relationality.

## Strongest objection

Aleksievich's oral history methodology has been criticized for methodological gaps and selection bias—researchers note that her interviewees' accounts are "unrepresentative" in their omission of important historical details (such as the murder of Jews in Belarus during WWII) and that her works blur the distinction between historical fact and literary construction, raising questions about whether polyphonic testimony adequately represents full historical complexity. Additionally, Kõresaar's postcolonial framework, while analytically sophisticated, has been challenged for potentially universalizing Estonian nationalist narratives without sufficient attention to how the postcolonial lens itself may obscure rather than illuminate the specific historical contexts of Baltic collaboration and resistance.

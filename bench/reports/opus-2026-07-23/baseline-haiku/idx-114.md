# **Analysis of the Competitive Landscape of Public Cloud Computing Services Market: IaaS and PaaS**

## **PART ONE: MARKET STRUCTURE AND COMPETITIVE DYNAMICS**

### Market Overview

The cloud computing services market—comprising Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) and Platform as a Service (PaaS)—has become critical infrastructure for modern business operations. The market is characterized by rapid growth and intense concentration among a small number of hyperscale providers.

In 2024, European cloud infrastructure service revenues (including IaaS, PaaS, and hosted private cloud services) reached €36 billion in the first half of the year, with the total European cloud market valued at €61 billion. The European cloud services market is forecast to grow at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 23%, reaching €110 billion by 2028. Globally, spending on cloud infrastructure services reached $78.2 billion in Q2 2024, growing 19% year-on-year.

### Main Hyperscale Providers and Key Players

The cloud computing market is dominated by three hyperscale providers that collectively control 70-80% of the global cloud market share:

1. **Amazon Web Services (AWS)** – The largest provider globally with 33% market share in Q2 2024
2. **Microsoft Azure** – The second-largest provider with 20% global market share in Q2 2024
3. **Google Cloud Platform (GCP)** – The third-largest provider with 10% global market share in Q2 2024

Beyond these three hyperscalers, the market includes smaller providers and regional players. In Europe, the collective market share of European cloud providers has declined from 29% in 2017 to approximately 15% in 2022, where it has remained relatively stable. In Asia-Pacific, regional players such as Naver Cloud and Kakao Enterprise compete alongside the hyperscalers, though global providers maintain dominant positions.

### Key Factors Leading to High Market Concentration

#### **1. Economies of Scale**

Hyperscale cloud providers benefit from substantial economies of scale that create structural barriers to competition:

- **Capital Requirements:** Establishing a single hyperscale data center campus requires $1–5 billion in capital investment and 18–36 months of construction time. These requirements effectively limit competition to well-capitalized incumbents.
- **Cost Advantages:** The ability to invest at such scale allows hyperscalers to realize substantial economies of scale, reducing the marginal costs of operating large-scale cloud services. This advantage compounds over time as larger players can spread infrastructure costs across a wider customer base.
- **Operational Economies:** Maintaining global infrastructure, regularly updating hardware, and continuously expanding service offerings require ongoing operational expenditures that smaller competitors struggle to sustain.

#### **2. Network Effects**

Network effects significantly amplify the dominance of established hyperscale providers:

**Developer Familiarity and Human Capital:** Developers preferentially learn and build expertise on the most widely-used cloud platforms. This developer experience advantage creates a self-reinforcing cycle where popular platforms attract more developers, which in turn attracts more customers seeking to hire developers familiar with those platforms.

**Independent Software Vendor (ISV) Partnerships:** The more applications that can run on a cloud platform, the greater value it generates for platform providers. ISV partner programs create collaborative business models where developers build applications that integrate with dominant cloud platforms. This network effect locks in applications and their developers to specific cloud ecosystems. The concentration of ISV activity on the largest platforms creates a moat against competing providers that lack equivalent ecosystem support.

**Ecosystem Lock-In:** Dominant cloud providers offer extensive tooling, documentation, sandbox environments, and technical support that attract ISVs. New entrants or smaller competitors cannot match these ecosystem investments, making it difficult for customers to achieve equivalent functionality on alternative platforms.

## **PART TWO: MAIN COMPETITION ISSUES AND ANTI-COMPETITIVE PRACTICES**

### **1. Switching Barriers**

Customers switching from one cloud provider to another face substantial technical and contractual obstacles that limit customer mobility and reduce competitive pressure on incumbent providers.

#### **Technical Barriers**

**Proprietary APIs and Data Formats:**
Cloud providers use proprietary application programming interfaces (APIs) and data formats that lack standardization across platforms. Applications built to use a specific cloud provider's API require substantial code modifications to migrate to a competing platform. These technical barriers include:

- Unique data formats that are not compatible across providers
- Proprietary service abstractions and interfaces
- Integration complexity with applications built around provider-specific features
- Lack of standardized interfaces across cloud platforms (though open standards like the Open Cloud Computing Interface (OCCI) exist, they have not achieved widespread adoption)

**Code and Architectural Rewriting:** Moving from one cloud provider to another often requires customers to rewrite significant portions of application code, re-architect infrastructure, and retrain internal teams on new tools and platforms. These costs scale with the complexity and size of the customer's cloud environment.

#### **Contractual and Financial Barriers**

**Volume Commitments and Long-Term Contracts:**
Cloud providers use long-term contracts with volume commitments that create financial disincentives for switching. Early termination penalties and minimum spending commitments bind customers to their initial provider choice.

**Egress Fees as Switching Costs:**
Egress fees operate as a structural switching cost. Data egress charges apply when customers transfer data from one cloud provider to another. According to the UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) final report (July 2025), egress fees create a "penalty for switching services." While AWS and Google announced in 2024 that they would eliminate egress fees, and Microsoft subsequently announced it would offer unrestricted free data transfers out of Azure for switching customers, these practices historically functioned as substantial exit costs.

The EU Data Act, which entered into force in January 2024 and has applied since September 2025, addresses this concern by requiring cloud providers to make customer data portable. From January 12, 2027, providers serving EU customers will be prohibited from charging any switching fees at all, including data egress charges incurred during a provider switch.

### **2. Pricing Issues**

#### **Egress Fees and Mark-Up Over Actual Costs**

Egress fees represent a significant competition concern because they function as switching barriers while also delivering high profit margins:

- Ingress is deliberately free (to attract customers), while egress is deliberately expensive (to trap customers)
- Egress fees are designed into the pricing model and operate as a structural feature rather than a cost-recovery mechanism
- Mark-up: Egress fees frequently exceed the actual cost of data transfer. Competition authorities have found these fees to be disproportionate to underlying costs.
- Impact: Customers with large data volumes face prohibitively high switching costs, effectively locking them into their initial provider regardless of service quality, pricing, or feature differences

#### **Cloud Credit Programs**

Large cloud credit programs targeting startups and other customer segments create complex competitive dynamics:

**Credit Magnitude:**
- AWS offers up to $100,000 in AWS service credits for startups
- Microsoft Azure offers up to $150,000 in credits over two years
- Google Cloud offers up to $200,000 in standard credits, and up to $350,000 for AI-first startups

**Competitive Effects:**
- **Loyalty Rebates:** Cloud credit programs function as conditional discounts that lock in early-stage customer relationships. Startups that build their infrastructure on one provider's platform using subsidized credits become dependent on that platform as they scale.
- **Disadvantage to Smaller Competitors:** Smaller cloud providers and new entrants cannot match the capital availability to offer equivalent credit programs, creating unequal competitive conditions.
- **Foreclosure Potential:** By offering substantial credits to startups and other customer categories, hyperscalers effectively foreclose market opportunities for smaller competitors who cannot match these subsidies.
- **Future Lock-In:** Today's seed-stage company receiving $250,000+ in credits potentially becomes tomorrow's enterprise customer with million-dollar monthly spending. Cloud providers recognize startups as early-stage versions of future enterprise customers and compete intensely for this early loyalty.

**Regulatory Response:**
France adopted the SREN Law on May 21, 2024, which strengthens oversight of cloud service providers and specifically regulates cloud credit programs by imposing limits on credit duration, prohibiting exclusivity clauses, and restricting tying practices.

### **3. Exclusive Licensing and Bundling**

#### **Microsoft's Bring Your Own License (BYOL) Policy Changes (2019)**

In October 2019, Microsoft implemented significant changes to its licensing terms that restricted the ability of customers to use existing licenses in cloud environments:

**Policy Change:**
Microsoft prohibited Bring Your Own License (BYOL) of on-premises licenses without Software Assurance on dedicated hosts within Amazon AWS, Google Cloud, Alibaba Cloud, and other third-party cloud providers. Historically, customers had been free to use on-premises licenses on dedicated servers hosted by or rented from cloud providers.

**Products Affected:**
Windows Server, SQL Server, and other enterprise software products were most significantly affected.

**Competitive Impact:**
- **Raised Competitor Costs:** The change raised the effective cost of using non-Microsoft cloud platforms by preventing customers from deploying existing Microsoft software licenses on competing providers' infrastructure without purchasing additional licensing.
- **Leverage into Azure:** The policy created an incentive for customers to migrate to Microsoft Azure, where on-premises licenses with Software Assurance could be used without restriction. This practice leveraged Microsoft's dominant market position in enterprise software into the competitive cloud services market.
- **Anti-Competitive Intent:** The timing and scope of this change—occurring after Azure growth stalls—suggest it was designed to promote Microsoft's cloud offering at the expense of competitors.

#### **Bundling of Services (Teams with Office 365)**

The European Commission found that Microsoft's bundling of Teams with Office 365 and Microsoft 365 constitutes an anti-competitive practice:

**Investigation and Findings:**
- The European Commission opened a competition probe in July 2023 in response to complaints from Slack and Alfaview.
- The Commission's preliminary findings (July 2025) concluded that Microsoft broke European Union antitrust rules by tying its cloud-based communications and collaboration software (Teams) to its online productivity suite (Office 365).

**Microsoft's Response and Insufficient Remedies:**
- Microsoft announced in October 2023 that it would unbundle Teams from Microsoft 365 and Office 365 in the EEA and Switzerland.
- Microsoft ceased selling Teams as a bundle globally in April 2024.
- **However:** The European Commission stated that these measures are insufficient to address competition concerns and further changes to Microsoft's conduct are necessary to restore competition.

**Competitive Impact:**
- **Distribution Leverage:** Bundling leverages Microsoft's dominant position in productivity software (Office 365 has established market penetration in enterprises and consumer markets) into the communications and collaboration software market.
- **Barrier to Competitors:** Bundling creates distribution advantages for Teams relative to standalone competitors like Slack, which must compete without the benefit of automatic inclusion in productivity suites.
- **Network Effects:** Bundling increases Teams' network effects by automatically providing it to all Office 365 customers, regardless of their preference for alternative communication platforms.

**Potential Penalties:**
The European Commission indicated that Microsoft could face fines of up to 10% of its global annual revenues for these violations.

## **Regulatory and Market Context**

### **European Union Digital Markets Act (DMA)**

In June 2025, the European Commission designated Amazon Web Services and Microsoft Azure as "gatekeepers" under the Digital Markets Act, despite these services not meeting the DMA's quantitative thresholds. The designation was based on:

- Scale, financial strength, and entrenched customer bases
- High switching costs and customer lock-in effects
- Functioning as an important gateway between businesses and their customers in the EU

Gatekeeper designation imposes obligations including limits on self-preferencing, requirements for interoperability, and mandates for data portability, with potential fines up to 10% of worldwide turnover for non-compliance.

### **UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) Findings**

The UK CMA concluded its cloud market investigation in July 2025, finding that:

- AWS and Microsoft's entrenched positions are stifling choice and innovation for British customers
- Market concentration concerns warrant investigation under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act (DMCC Act), enacted in May 2024
- Key concerns include cloud migration costs (egress fees), software licensing practices, and customer lock-in effects

### **OECD Analysis (May 2025)**

The OECD's "Competition in the Provision of Cloud Computing Services" report (2025) identified that cloud markets are characterized by:

- High concentration among hyperscale providers
- Significant switching barriers (both technical and contractual)
- Leveraging practices (exclusive licensing, bundling, cloud credits)
- Underdeveloped interoperability limiting customer portability
- Additional complexity from cloud providers' dual role as both infrastructure suppliers and AI service competitors

## **Conclusion**

The public cloud computing services market exhibits structural characteristics that are conducive to market concentration and reduced competition. High capital requirements, substantial economies of scale, and powerful network effects create barriers to entry that favor established hyperscale providers. Technical lock-in through proprietary APIs and data formats, combined with contractual and financial switching barriers such as egress fees and volume commitments, limit customer mobility and competitive pressure.

Anti-competitive practices—including restrictive licensing policies (Microsoft's BYOL restrictions), bundling of services (Teams with Office 365), and large-scale cloud credit programs—further entrench the market positions of dominant players. These practices leverage market power in existing markets into adjacent markets and create barriers for smaller competitors.

Competition authorities in the EU, UK, France, and OECD have identified these structural and behavioral concerns as warranting regulatory intervention. Recent regulatory actions, including the EU DMA gatekeeper designations, the Data Act's data portability requirements, and the French SREN Law's restrictions on cloud credit programs, represent attempts to restore competitive dynamics in this critical infrastructure market.

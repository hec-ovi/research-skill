## Summary

Three hyperscalers, Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud, dominate global IaaS/PaaS spending (63% combined per Synergy Research, Q3 2025), with national market studies showing even higher concentration domestically: AWS held 46% of French IaaS/PaaS revenue in 2021, Azure held 40-45% of the Dutch market in 2020, AWS and Azure combined held 70-80% of the UK market in 2022, AWS held 62.1% of South Korea's market in 2021, and Japan's top three providers combined held 60-70% by FY2020. Competition authorities in France, the Netherlands, the UK, South Korea, and Japan, plus the US FTC and EU Commission, independently converge on the same three concerns: technical and contractual switching barriers, egress fees and cloud credits that function as lock-in mechanisms, and exclusionary software licensing and bundling, particularly by Microsoft. The UK CMA's July 2025 final decision found three formal Adverse Effects on Competition and recommended Strategic Market Status investigations into Microsoft and AWS; the EU Commission separately made a preliminary finding in June 2026 that AWS and Azure should be designated DMA gatekeepers. AWS and Microsoft have publicly rejected these findings as based on "hypothetical" and "theoretical" scenarios in an otherwise "competitive, well-functioning" market, a rebuttal not shared by smaller cloud providers that responded to the same UK inquiry.

## Findings

### Part One: Market Structure and Competitive Dynamics

The global public cloud infrastructure market is dominated by three "hyperscale" providers: Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud. According to Synergy Research Group's Q3 2025 tracking, the three companies together accounted for 63% of enterprise cloud infrastructure spending worldwide, with Amazon at 29%, Microsoft at 20%, and Google at 13%; Google's share is described as "nearly four times the size of fourth-placed Alibaba." Other key players include Oracle, Alibaba Cloud, and a newer cohort of GPU-focused "neoclouds" (CoreWeave, Crusoe, Nebius, Lambda), alongside national and regional providers that retain meaningful positions in specific markets: Naver Cloud in South Korea, NTT Communications, Fujitsu, and Sakura Internet in Japan, and OVHcloud, Deutsche Telekom, and SAP in Europe.

Regulatory classification of the hyperscalers has advanced alongside market-share findings. On 25 June 2026 the European Commission reached a preliminary position that AWS and Azure, which it described as "the largest and second largest cloud computing services in the EU respectively," should be designated gatekeepers under the Digital Markets Act, citing their "vast and entrenched user bases," resulting "lock-in effects and high switching costs," and AI-driven ecosystem partnerships that have become "a decisive factor in cloud procurement." In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission has sent civil investigative demands to at least half a dozen of Microsoft's competitors as part of a formal probe (reported February 2026) into whether Microsoft "makes it difficult (or more costly) for customers to run its software on third-party cloud platforms." In the United Kingdom, the Competition and Markets Authority concluded on 31 July 2025 that "competition is not working well" in public cloud infrastructure, identifying three formal Adverse Effects on Competition and recommending Strategic Market Status investigations into Microsoft and AWS.

The table reflects what each national study actually published rather than a uniform breakdown, and the gaps are themselves informative. The French Autorité de la concurrence's opinion 23-A-08 states plainly that "the two leading providers, Amazon and Microsoft, will have captured 46% and 17% respectively of revenues from IaaS and PaaS services in 2021," with Google Cloud's 8% coming from the Autorité's earlier press release on the same study; the Autorité adds that the three hyperscalers "captured 80% of the growth in spending on public cloud infrastructures and applications" that year but does not itself state a residual "other" figure, so the ~29% here is arithmetic, not a quoted statistic. The Dutch Authority for Consumers and Markets (ACM), drawing on revenue data it gathered directly from nine operators, put Azure at 40-45%, AWS at 30-35%, and Google Cloud (GCP) at 5-10% of the Dutch market in 2020. Ofcom's UK cloud services market study (final report, October 2023) reported AWS and Microsoft's 2022 UK share only as a combined figure of 70-80%, with Google as "closest competitor" at 5-10%; Ofcom did not publish an individual AWS/Azure split for 2022, though the CMA's later 2024 data (in its July 2025 final decision) shows AWS and Microsoft essentially at parity, each at 30-40% of IaaS. South Korea's Fair Trade Commission (KFTC) surveyed 32 major cloud providers and roughly 3,000 customer, distribution-partner, and marketplace-solution firms; its published trend shows AWS falling from 77.9% (2019) to 70.0% (2020) to 62.1% (2021), while Microsoft rose from 6.7% to 9.4% to 12.0% over the same years, and Naver Cloud displaced Google Cloud for third place from 2020 onward (Google held 3.5% in 2019). Japan's Fair Trade Commission (JFTC), in its June 2022 "Report on Trade Practices in Cloud Services Sector," deliberately discloses company-level shares only as bands to protect commercially sensitive data; its own chart shows the "Three CSPs" (AWS, Microsoft, Google) rising from a combined 50-60% of the market in FY2017 to 60-70% in FY2020, alongside a rising Herfindahl-Hirschman Index that the JFTC itself describes as evidence "the degree of market concentration in the IaaS and PaaS markets is increasing every year."

**Factors Leading to High Market Concentration**

Economies of scale anchor the concentration these studies describe. The French Autorité identifies "high fixed costs" and "economies of scale" as structural features of the sector, noting that "hyperscalers enjoy considerable financial muscle, enabling them to make extremely substantial investments" and concluding that "the likelihood of a new operator being able to gain market share rapidly appears limited, apart from operators who are already powerful in other digital markets." The Dutch ACM reaches the same conclusion through infrastructure sharing: "by sharing physical hardware, organizations found it easier to scale up and scale down in terms of server capacity and these servers could be used more efficiently, leading to considerable savings," adding that "other cloud providers cannot keep up with the growth and investment pace of the major cloud providers."

Network effects reinforce the same dynamic through two related channels: independent software vendors (ISVs) and developer familiarity. The Centre on Regulation in Europe (CERRE), in its February 2024 economic assessment of EU cloud policy, describes a self-reinforcing loop: because "the technology, software, and APIs of the cloud providers can differ," ISVs find it costly to support more than one platform and so "typically develop services for one or a few cloud providers, notably the bigger ones," while "cloud providers with the most customers attract the most ISVs, creating ecosystems around a few cloud providers." CERRE also flags a competitive tension in this arrangement: ISVs that build on a hyperscaler's infrastructure often simultaneously compete against that same hyperscaler's own services, which "can raise concerns about a competitive level playing field." The French Autorité makes a parallel point about direct customer network effects, observing that hyperscalers "have access to a preexisting customer base that enables them to take advantage of significant network effects, and which can be used as leverage to expand rapidly," and that "large hyperscalers organised into ecosystems enjoy competitive advantages over suppliers offering more limited catalogues of services." Developer familiarity operates as its own network effect: the Dutch ACM notes that "using the services of large cloud providers offers the best chance of finding developers," which matters for customers seeking support and for ISVs pursuing "the ongoing development of their business," while Japan's JFTC found in its own customer survey that 23.1% of IaaS customers and 20.5% of PaaS customers cited the availability of "a large number of introduction support providers and engineers...who can deal with that cloud service" as a reason for choosing their current provider.

Integration with other digital services extends this advantage into adjacent markets. The French Autorité's opinion flags the risk that "a software publisher who happens to be a cloud service provider could implement practices aimed at raising the price of the licenses needed by its competitors," and that hyperscalers' "conglomerate structure" lets them "develop discount systems, tariff and non-tariff benefits or cross-subsidies" that favor their own cloud offering. The EU Commission's June 2026 preliminary gatekeeper position makes the same point at the level of AI: it cites hyperscalers' "large ecosystems with AI tools and partnerships" as having become "a decisive factor in cloud procurement," compounding the entrenchment created by scale and network effects.

### Part Two: Main Competition Issues and Anti-Competitive Practices

**1. Switching Barriers**

Technical barriers to switching center on non-standard APIs and proprietary data formats. The Dutch ACM's market study is explicit on this mechanism: "if the cloud provider of a specific service has not disclosed the APIs and standards, the service that wishes to connect will not know how this service works," so that "data cannot always be transferred (properly or at all) due to the use of different, closed APIs and standards; data portability is therefore not always possible." The ACM further identifies "native cloud services" as a distinct lock-in vector: "a native cloud service of cloud provider A works only in the cloud environment of cloud provider A. This service cannot run in other cloud environments, because the necessary licenses have not been made available." Japan's JFTC found the same obstacle from the customer side, reporting that "the high transfer fee for migration of accumulated data is a hurdle to switching cloud services" alongside technical incompatibility with data formats, and separately found that 85.9% of surveyed customers (263 of 480) said they would keep using their current provider even if its price rose 5-10%, evidence the JFTC frames as switching frictions outweighing price sensitivity. The French Autorité, in the same opinion, describes "technological barriers to migration" arising from "the variety of products and services, especially PaaS services," where "lack of portability of data and applications can lead to significant migration costs."

Contractual and financial barriers compound the technical ones. The Dutch ACM points to per-unit billing complexity: "for every action, stored GB or second of computing power, users have to pay," a structure that "creates unpredictability about the total costs" and makes it difficult for customers to compare providers or anticipate the financial consequences of switching. South Korea's KFTC survey found that 79.9% of surveyed companies spend 60% or more of their cloud budget with a single provider, and that among companies that found switching difficult, 76.7% cited "the cost burden and technical limits of data transfer" as the primary reason; separately, 68% of Korean respondents said they had no plans to adopt a multi-cloud strategy at all. The UK CMA's July 2025 final decision treats switching and multi-cloud barriers as one of its three formal Adverse Effects on Competition, concluding that technical and commercial barriers "lock customers into their initial choice of provider, which may not reflect their evolving needs," and separately found that Microsoft's software licensing practices constitute their own, distinct AEC (addressed under licensing below).

**2. Pricing Issues**

Egress fees are identified across every jurisdiction studied as a direct financial penalty for switching, distinct from the technical barriers above. The French Autorité found that "egress fees are potentially disconnected from the costs directly incurred by suppliers regarding data transfers" and that, "as they are currently structured, these fees could create a risk of customer lock-in on a fast-growing market, by making it more difficult for cloud users to leave their primary provider." The Dutch ACM reached the same conclusion from a comparative angle: "the egress fees of the major cloud providers are much higher than those of most smaller providers. As a result, the user has to make a large investment if he wants to move his data to another provider," a structure that follows from cloud providers charging nothing to move data in ("ingress") while charging specifically to move it out. South Korea's KFTC characterized this same asymmetry as a deliberate industry strategy: providers have "a structure of charging no inbound fees for receiving data but receiving outbound charges for transmitting data." The UK CMA's final decision similarly treats egress fees as "a key commercial barrier," finding that their "presence and magnitude...reduce the ability and incentives for customers to switch or multi-cloud, particularly smaller customers and those with large proportions of stored data for whom fees are highest relative to their total cloud spending," though the CMA's published decision redacts the specific cost-to-price markup it examined. Independent analysis by Cloudflare quantifies the gap the regulators describe only qualitatively: industry-wide wholesale internet transit prices fell by an average of 23% per year over the preceding decade, while AWS's egress fees over the same period fell by only about 25% in total, and "since 2018, the egress fees AWS charges in North America and Europe have not dropped a penny even as wholesale prices in those markets over the same time period have fallen by more than half"; Cloudflare's regional markup analysis puts South Korea's markup specifically at 357% over wholesale transit cost. As of 2025-2026, list prices for egress to the public internet across AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud sit in the range of roughly $0.085-$0.09 per gigabyte. The EU Data Act, in force since 11 January 2024, has since that date restricted switching charges (including egress fees) to providers' direct costs of assisting a transition, and from 12 January 2027 will prohibit switching charges, including data egress charges, entirely for EU customers, a remedy several law firms tracking the Act (including Mishcon de Reya and Kemp IT Law) describe as targeted specifically at removing this lock-in mechanism.

Cloud credit programs raise a parallel concern about disadvantaging smaller competitors rather than customers directly. The French Autorité distinguishes two categories: small trial credits "granted by almost all cloud service providers," ranging "from a few dozen euros to a thousand euros" and lasting "no more than three months," and much larger "support programs" credits "offered mainly by larger cloud service providers for users with high innovation potential, such as start-ups," covering "hundreds of thousands of euros" and lasting "several years." The Autorité's concern is squarely about lock-in: "given the time-consuming and costly developments required by customers to set up a cloud architecture with a specific provider, and the technical and financial costs associated with migration, there is a risk of lock-in by the major providers," and it notes pointedly that the scale of these programs raises "doubts about the ability of all cloud providers to offer them profitably" — i.e., that only the largest, best-capitalized hyperscalers can sustain credit programs at this scale, which structurally disadvantages smaller rivals trying to compete for the same early-stage customers. The Dutch ACM documents the same mechanism with a concrete figure, noting some startup/scale-up credit programs reach "as much as 150,000 US dollars," and observes that this is precisely "at that first moment of choice" that competition for a customer's initial architecture decision is most intense, after which switching becomes costly. Current programs bear this out: as of 2026, AWS Activate offers up to $100,000 in standard startup credits (and up to $300,000 for frontier AI model developers), Microsoft's Founders Hub offers up to $150,000 through a staged, milestone-based structure that does not require prior venture backing, and Google Cloud offers up to $100,000 for funded startups and up to $350,000 for AI-focused startups with TPU access.

**3. Exclusive Licensing and Bundling**

Microsoft changed the terms under which customers could bring their own on-premises software licenses into the cloud effective 1 October 2019, announced the preceding August. Historically, customers holding on-premises licenses for products such as Windows Server and SQL Server could deploy them on any cloud provider's dedicated hosted infrastructure without an added licensing cost. Under the 2019 change, licenses purchased without Software Assurance mobility rights lost that portability specifically on a defined set of "Listed Providers": Microsoft Azure, Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud, and Alibaba Cloud; contemporaneous reporting from Computerworld and licensing advisory firms including apps4rent described the change as closing what Microsoft characterized as a "loophole" that let customers avoid paying twice for the same functionality, while critics characterized it as "leav[ing] cloud rivals high and dry." The UK CMA's July 2025 final decision, in a formal Adverse Effects on Competition finding made six years after that policy took effect, confirms the commercial mechanism critics described: "Microsoft does not make certain products available to AWS and Google through their licensing agreements," and "customers with existing licences cannot bring these to AWS and Google in most instances." The CMA's own price analysis goes further, finding a genuine cost-raising effect on rivals rather than merely an inconvenience: "the input price paid to Microsoft by AWS and Google for some of these products can be higher than Microsoft's customer-facing price for some cloud customers," and concluding that these practices are "adversely impacting the competitiveness of AWS and Google in the supply of cloud services." The scale of the resulting price gap has been reported concretely in connection with the FTC's parallel 2026 investigation, which examined claims that it can cost roughly four times as much to run Windows Server on AWS, Alibaba, or Google Cloud as on Azure itself.

Bundling of complementary software has separately drawn a formal antitrust finding against Microsoft. The European Commission, in case AT.40873 under Article 102 TFEU, found that bundling Microsoft Teams with Office 365 and Microsoft 365 commercial subscriptions gave Teams a "distribution advantage" that foreclosed rivals, specifically Slack and Zoom, from competing for the same customers on equal terms; the case originated from a Slack complaint in 2020 and proceeded through a formal Statement of Objections before Microsoft offered revised commitments in May 2025. The Commission accepted Microsoft's binding commitments on 12 September 2025, under which Microsoft must offer versions of Office 365 and Microsoft 365 without Teams at an "appreciably lower price," give customers "recurrent opportunities" to switch to a Teams-free suite, ensure interoperability for key functions between Teams and rival tools, and allow users to export their Teams data for use in competing products; the commitments run for seven years. The underlying competitive logic mirrors the licensing findings above: bundling a dominant productivity suite with a communications tool leverages market power built in one market (productivity software) to gain a distribution advantage in an adjacent one (workplace communications), independent of Teams' standalone merits, exactly as Microsoft's licensing terms leverage its position in server and productivity software to raise effective costs for rival IaaS providers.

## Insights

- Every mechanism examined here operates through the same structural logic: raise the customer's cost of leaving rather than compete solely on the customer's cost of staying. Egress fees whose price trend has decoupled from a decade of falling wholesale transit costs (Cloudflare), licensing terms that make a competitor's infrastructure four times more expensive to run the same software on (FTC/Register reporting on Microsoft), and cloud credits sized specifically to cover the multi-year cost of switching later (French Autorité, Dutch ACM) are three different instruments aimed at the identical outcome, which is why five separate national studies converge on naming all three despite differing regulatory traditions.
- Scale-driven advantages and lock-in mechanisms are mutually reinforcing rather than independent: economies of scale fund the credit programs and cross-subsidized bundles (Autorité's "conglomerate structure" point) that establish a customer relationship, and network effects through ISVs and developer familiarity (CERRE, ACM, JFTC) then make that relationship progressively more expensive to unwind, meaning the barriers do not simply add up but compound over a customer's lifecycle.
- The trajectory across jurisdictions is toward remedy, not merely findings: Japan's JFTC (2022), France's Autorité (2023), the Netherlands' ACM (2022), South Korea's KFTC (2022), the UK CMA (2025), and the EU Commission's Teams case (2025) and gatekeeper designation (2026) all post-date the market conditions they describe by one to five years, and the UK and EU have both moved from market study to binding intervention (CMA's recommended Strategic Market Status process; the EU Data Act's phased egress-fee ban; Microsoft's binding seven-year Teams commitments), indicating the diagnostic phase across major markets is largely complete and enforcement is the current frontier.
- Where the underlying data is genuinely comparable across countries, the concentration trend is consistent even though absolute shares vary: AWS's home-market share has been falling in South Korea (77.9% to 62.1%, 2019-2021) while Microsoft's has been rising there (6.7% to 12.0%), the same relative pattern the UK CMA finds in its own market (AWS declining, Microsoft growing, since 2020) and that Synergy Research finds globally (Amazon's share "gradually eroded" while Microsoft and Google gain); this suggests Azure's productivity-suite and licensing bundle, not raw infrastructure competitiveness, is the more likely driver of Microsoft's relative gains, consistent with the CMA's and Commission's licensing and bundling findings rather than contradicting them.
- The Japan and UK 2022 table cells being unavailable as individual company splits, while South Korea's are fully disclosed, is itself a data point about regulatory disclosure norms: the JFTC deliberately bands company-level shares to protect confidential business information even in a public antitrust report, while the KFTC published exact percentages down to a tenth of a point, a divergence in transparency practice that likely also shapes how contestable each government's evidence base is to outside challenge.

## Strongest objection

AWS and Microsoft have directly rejected the UK CMA's findings that underpin much of this report. AWS called the CMA's proposed intervention "not warranted" and described the regulator's approach as "unwarranted intervention applicable to only two players in one of the most competitive, well-functioning, and fast-growing sectors of the UK economy," while Microsoft said the CMA "grounds much of its analysis in hypothetical scenarios and focuses on largely theoretical issues" and, on the final decision, that the regulator had "missed the mark again" in a market that "has never been so dynamic and competitive, with record investment and rapid, AI-driven changes." This objection is weakened, though not eliminated, by the fact that smaller cloud providers responding to the same CMA inquiry broadly endorsed the watchdog's findings rather than sharing AWS and Microsoft's assessment, suggesting the disagreement tracks incumbency rather than a genuine split in expert opinion about the state of competition.

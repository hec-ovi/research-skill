## Summary

Three independent systematic reviews (Tsai et al. 2022, Yin et al. 2022, and a 2022 Cochrane review) that pooled RCTs on salt substitutes published up to March 2022 converge on the same picture: potassium-enriched salt substitutes lower systolic BP by roughly 4.6-4.8 mmHg and diastolic BP by roughly 1.5-2.4 mmHg versus regular salt, and reduce all-cause mortality by about 11-12% and cardiovascular events by a similar margin, driven heavily by the 20,995-person SSaSS trial in China. The BP-lowering effect alone, run through established blood-pressure-outcome meta-analyses, predicts roughly 9-13% reductions in stroke and major cardiovascular events — closely matching what SSaSS actually observed (14% and 13% respectively) — while the observed mortality benefit runs somewhat larger than BP alone would predict. Effect modification is real but selective: the BP benefit is more pronounced in trials of people ≥60 years and in smaller trials (<300 participants), but not meaningfully different between trials under vs. over 12 months. The core caveat, flagged by every review, is that all trials excluded people with kidney disease or hyperkalemia risk, so nothing here speaks to safety in populations with undiagnosed CKD.

Rather than take that table on faith, a subset of the largest and most consequential trials were traced to their own primary publications: Neal and colleagues' NEJM report of SSaSS confirms the 20,995-participant cluster design, the 75%/25% NaCl/KCl substitute, and the 4.74-year mean follow-up; Chang and colleagues' report in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition confirms the 768-versus-1213 split among Taiwanese veterans and the 49%/49% substitute; Li and colleagues' 2016 report in PLOS One confirms the 18-month, ~1,295-participant cluster trial and its explicit statement that "baseline survey data were not available"; Yu and colleagues' 2021 report of the Salt Substitute in India Study confirms the 502-participant, 70%/30% substitute, 3-month design; and Sun and colleagues' exploratory 10-year follow-up paper confirms the 209-versus-219 mortality cohort and its baseline blood pressures. Each of these matched the Tsai table's entries. Two additional independent aggregators, Yin and colleagues' review in Heart (search through 31 August 2021, 21 trials, 31,949 participants) and a Cochrane review of low-sodium salt substitutes (search completed 18 August 2021, 26 trials, 34,961 adults), draw on a substantially overlapping but not identical trial set and were used to cross-check pooled effect sizes rather than the row-by-row table.

**Overall significance for blood pressure control and mortality.** All three reviews land on effect sizes that agree to within a fraction of a millimeter of mercury. Tsai and colleagues reported a pooled systolic BP mean difference of -4.80 mmHg (95% CI -6.12 to -3.48, P<0.0001) and diastolic mean difference of -1.48 mmHg (95% CI -2.06 to -0.90, P<0.0001) across 21 trials reporting blood pressure. Yin and colleagues reported -4.61 mmHg (95% CI -6.07 to -3.14) systolic and -1.61 mmHg (95% CI -2.42 to -0.79) diastolic across 19 blood-pressure trials. The Cochrane review reported -4.76 mmHg systolic and -2.43 mmHg diastolic, both graded moderate-certainty evidence. Yin's review adds a dose-response finding: each 10-percentage-point reduction in the proportion of sodium chloride in the substitute was associated with a further -1.53 mmHg systolic and -0.95 mmHg diastolic reduction, consistent with a genuine sodium-for-potassium substitution mechanism rather than a nonspecific trial effect.

On mortality, Tsai's pooled analysis of five outcome trials found all-cause mortality reduced by a hazard ratio of 0.88 (95% CI 0.82-0.94, P=0.0003), a 12% relative reduction, while cardiovascular death showed a hazard ratio of 0.72 (95% CI 0.52-1.00, P=0.05) — directionally consistent but sitting exactly at the conventional significance threshold. Yin's pooled analysis found total mortality risk ratio 0.89 (95% CI 0.85-0.94, an 11% reduction), cardiovascular mortality risk ratio 0.87 (95% CI 0.81-0.94, a 13% reduction), and cardiovascular events risk ratio 0.89 (95% CI 0.85-0.94, an 11% reduction). The Cochrane review, using GRADE, characterized non-fatal stroke, non-fatal acute coronary syndrome, and cardiovascular mortality as each "probably" reduced "slightly," at moderate certainty. Almost all of the mortality signal is carried by SSaSS: in that trial alone, stroke fell from 33.65 to 29.14 events per 1,000 person-years (rate ratio 0.86, 95% CI 0.77-0.96, a 14% reduction), major cardiovascular events fell from 56.29 to 49.09 per 1,000 person-years (rate ratio 0.87, 95% CI 0.80-0.94, a 13% reduction), and death fell from 44.61 to 39.28 per 1,000 person-years (rate ratio 0.88, 95% CI 0.82-0.95, a 12% reduction), with serum-potassium-attributed serious adverse events not significantly different between arms. Chang and colleagues' Taiwan trial, run over three decades earlier in a very different population of institutionalized elderly veterans, found a cardiovascular mortality hazard ratio of 0.59 (95% CI 0.37-0.95, roughly a 41% relative reduction) but no significant reduction in all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 0.90, 95% CI 0.79-1.06). Serum potassium itself was not meaningfully elevated by salt substitution in the pooled data (Tsai: mean difference 0.10 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.02 to 0.23, P=0.10; Cochrane: mean difference +0.12 mmol/L, moderate certainty, hyperkalemia "little to no difference").

**Subgroup analyses.** Tsai and colleagues' pre-specified subgroups map directly onto the three splits requested. By age, the systolic BP-lowering effect was significantly more pronounced among trials enrolling participants aged 60 years and older than in younger populations, and heterogeneity was lower within the older-age subgroup. By sample size, trials with fewer than 300 participants showed a larger-magnitude systolic BP reduction than trials with 300 or more participants. By duration, the review explicitly reported no significant effect modification: trials under 12 months (ranging from 4 weeks to 9 months in this dataset) and trials of 12 months or longer (ranging up to 10 years, as in Sun and colleagues' follow-up) produced comparable blood-pressure effects, meaning the benefit neither faded nor grew with longer exposure in the pooled data. Geography was tested as a fourth stratifier and also showed no significant effect modification, with Tsai's authors stating explicitly that Asian and non-Asian trial results were comparable. Overall statistical heterogeneity for the systolic BP outcome remained high (the Cochrane review reported I² of 78-88% for blood-pressure outcomes), and Tsai's authors attributed it to differences in blinding, follow-up duration, sample size, and salt-substitute formulation, of which age-stratification was the single factor that most reduced heterogeneity.

**Estimated cardiovascular risk reduction from the observed SBP-lowering effect.** This is a derived estimate, not a number reported directly by any single salt-substitute trial. A 2016 Lancet meta-analysis of blood-pressure-lowering trials (123 studies, 613,815 participants) found that each 10 mmHg reduction in systolic BP reduces major cardiovascular events by a relative risk of 0.80 (20%), coronary heart disease by 0.83 (17%), stroke by 0.73 (27%), heart failure by roughly 28%, and all-cause mortality by 13%. The Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment Trialists' Collaboration's 2021 Lancet individual-participant-data meta-analysis (48 trials, 344,716 participants) found that each 5 mmHg reduction, sustained over an average of four years, cuts major cardiovascular events by about 10%, stroke by about 13%, heart failure by about 13%, ischaemic heart disease by about 8%, and cardiovascular death by about 5%. Applying these benchmarks to the pooled salt-substitute effect of roughly -4.6 to -4.8 mmHg systolic — close to the BPLTTC's 5 mmHg reference point — predicts approximately a 9-13% reduction in stroke and major cardiovascular events and roughly a 5-6% reduction in all-cause mortality attributable to the BP pathway alone. The stroke prediction lines up closely with what SSaSS actually measured (14% observed versus roughly 12-13% predicted from BP alone), but the observed mortality reductions across the pooled trials (11-12%) run noticeably above what BP lowering alone would predict (roughly 5-6%), an gap addressed further below.

## Insights

- The three reviews used non-identical trial sets and search cutoffs (Tsai: PubMed/Cochrane through March 2022, 23 trials; Yin: through August 2021, 21 trials; Cochrane: through August 2021, 26 trials) yet converged on systolic BP effects within 0.2 mmHg of one another (-4.61 to -4.80 mmHg). That convergence across differently-assembled trial sets, rather than any single review's internal consistency, is what makes the blood-pressure effect credible.
- Yin's dose-response finding — greater BP reduction as the sodium-chloride share of the substitute drops — is a mechanistic signature that a placebo or attention effect would not produce, and it strengthens the causal reading of the pooled trial data.
- Triangulating the indirect (BP-mediated) and direct (hard-outcome) evidence chains largely agrees for stroke (roughly 12-13% predicted from BP-lowering benchmarks versus 14% observed in SSaSS) but diverges for mortality (roughly 5-6% predicted versus 11-12% observed pooled, and a 41% cardiovascular-mortality reduction in Chang's much smaller Taiwan trial). Because potassium itself has antiarrhythmic and vascular effects independent of blood pressure, this gap is consistent with salt substitution conferring cardiovascular benefit through a channel beyond systolic BP lowering alone, not solely through the BP pathway.
- The subgroup pattern (stronger effect in older participants and in smaller trials, no attenuation by duration) has a plausible non-biological explanation alongside a biological one: smaller trials more often enrolled frailer, higher-baseline-BP populations, and Tsai's own analysis detected significant funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger's test P<0.05) for the BP outcomes, meaning small-study/publication-bias effects likely inflate part of the apparent age and sample-size effect modification rather than reflecting a purely biological age-dependent response.
- Li and colleagues' 2016 China trial is the clearest outlier in the whole dataset: a comparably large, 18-month, community health-education-based salt-substitution program produced no significant BP change (systolic mean difference -1.1 mmHg, P=0.33), in contrast to trials like SSaSS and Chang's that fully replaced household salt supply directly. That gap suggests the pooled efficacy-trial effect, built on trials that provided free substitute salt for complete replacement, may not transfer one-for-one to population programs relying on voluntary uptake and behavioral education.
- Chang's 2006 Taiwan trial (institutionalized, fully diet-controlled elderly veterans) and Yu's 2021 India trial (free-living hypertensive outpatients managing their own household cooking) sit at opposite ends of the delivery-model spectrum yet produced BP and mortality effects of similar direction and order of magnitude, arguing that the benefit is not an artifact of one particular feeding or delivery method.

## Strongest objection

Every trial in every review excluded people with known chronic kidney disease, potassium-sparing medication use, or other conditions that predispose to hyperkalemia, and even the largest and longest trial, SSaSS, did not perform serial potassium monitoring, according to critiques of the trial and the Cochrane review's own stated limitation. Because undiagnosed CKD is common and unscreened in the general population, the demonstrated safety (no significant increase in hyperkalemia-attributed serious adverse events) cannot be extended to real-world, unscreened rollout; combine that with most trials being open-label (unblinded), GRADE-rated only "moderate" (not high) certainty by Cochrane, substantial statistical heterogeneity (I² 78-88% for blood pressure), and a rural-Chinese-diet setting where home-cooked salt dominates sodium intake in a way that may not generalize to Western populations reliant on processed food, and the case for population-wide salt-substitute rollout is considerably less settled than the pooled point estimates alone suggest.

## Summary

Ene Kõresaar's case study of Boris Takk, an Estonian Waffen-SS conscript turned secret Communist Party member turned post-1991 "memory entrepreneur," shows him using the postcolonial concepts of "prolonged rupture" and a "third way" to recast fifty years of contradictory choices as one unbroken fight for Estonian freedom; Kõresaar reads this as exemplifying Estonia's dominant postcolonial memory narrative of dual colonization by Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union. Svetlana Aleksievich's *War's Unwomanly Face* pursues a contrasting bottom-up "history of feelings," built from Soviet women veterans' oral testimony. Jasmina Husanović's "politics of witnessing and hope" rejects both nationalist memory-ownership and liberal transitional justice, seeking civil-society and art-based openings instead. Holocaust historians (notably Dovid Katz) argue the exact dual-colonization framing Kõresaar identifies is the intellectual core of the contested "Double Genocide" paradigm, which risks relativizing the Holocaust and rehabilitating collaborators.

## Findings

**Part One: The Case of Boris Takk (as studied by Ene Kõresaar)**

Boris Takk's story appears in Kõresaar's edited collection *Soldiers of Memory: World War II and Its Aftermath in Estonian Post-Soviet Life Stories* (Rodopi, 2011), in her own chapter "Boris Takk – The Ambiguity of War in a Post-Soviet Life Story." As a teenager he was caught up in the 1943 mobilization that channeled young Estonian men into the Estonian Legion, the Waffen-SS volunteer formation Nazi Germany permitted after 1942 recruitment pressure; he later described joining partly to fight "the hated Communists" and to avenge family losses to Soviet terror. He then remained in Soviet-occupied Estonia through the full 1944–1991 occupation, and in the early 1970s joined the Communist Party of Estonia. He recorded his life story for Kõresaar's project decades later, prompted, per her account, by his mother's death and reflections on the passage of time, casting himself as both witness to history and spokesperson for his generation.

After Estonia regained independence in 1991, Takk became politically active as what Kõresaar calls a memory entrepreneur: he organized gatherings of fellow Legion veterans and campaigned publicly for his version of historical truth, on the premise that Estonians could not lead a "normal" national life without their own state.

The organizing device of his narrative is the postcolonial concept of "prolonged rupture": he treats the entire 1940–1991 span, first Soviet occupation, Nazi occupation, second Soviet occupation, as one continuous abnormal period in which invading foreign powers destroyed the Estonian way of life, rather than as three morally distinct episodes. Within that frame he presents his Waffen-SS service and his later, secret Party membership as two tactical moments of a single unbroken project, describing his wartime choice as "a continuous fight for Estonia's freedom, against the Communists." He resolves the moral weight of having worn a German uniform through "third way" reasoning: Estonia's real choice was to endure and outlast both occupiers rather than side with either. He extended the identical logic to his Party years, telling Kõresaar directly that he "wanted to serve the Estonian cause from inside the system."

Among the eight life stories Kõresaar collected, Takk's was the only one to address the Holocaust, and he did so relatively openly for the early 1990s, when the subject was not yet widely discussed in Estonia's public sphere: he spoke of the "disappearance" of Estonian Jews and described the Klooga camp, where German forces and local collaborators murdered roughly 2,000 Jews in September 1944, as "a dismal site." This specific detail traces to a single identifiable analytic source in this research (Kõresaar's chapter as reported by Dovilė Budrytė) and could not be independently corroborated from a second unrelated origin, so it should be read as well-attested but single-sourced rather than doubly confirmed.

Kõresaar treats Takk's account as more than personal rationalization: in her analysis it exemplifies "the dominant view of history that evaluates the entire history of Estonia in the twentieth century from a postcolonial perspective," under which World War II and its aftermath are read as dual colonization, first by Nazi Germany, then, more lastingly, by the Soviet Union. Karl Stuklis's 2024 comparative study of Estonian and Latvian Waffen-SS memoirs, published in the *Journal of Baltic Studies*, independently confirms that "prolonged rupture" is Kõresaar's term for a pattern recurring across post-Soviet Estonian war narratives generally, not confined to Takk. A review of *Soldiers of Memory* in Baltic Worlds adds that the volume works to "subvert the 'ethnicization'" of Estonia's war memory, using its cover image, two Estonian soldiers in German and Soviet uniform shown as friends, to visualize this same dual subjection.

**Part Two: The Perspectives of Svetlana Aleksievich and Jasmina Husanović**

In *War's Unwomanly Face* (1985), Svetlana Aleksievich set out to write, in her words, "not the history of war, but the history of feelings," what the Nobel Committee later called the work of "a historian of the soul" rather than a military chronicler. Interviewing roughly a million Soviet women who served in combat, medical, and partisan roles during World War II, contributions largely erased from official Soviet historiography, she built the book from oral testimony, letting war appear through "its own colours, its own smells, its own space and its own words," including bodily and emotional detail (hunger, fear, menstruation, acts of compassion toward enemy soldiers and prisoners) excluded from state-sanctioned heroic accounts. She treated her narrators as active creators of their own history rather than passive informants, and registered silence itself as testimony, including one veteran's flat refusal, "I do not want to remember." The book directly countered the Soviet "Great Patriotic War" myth of uniform, gender-neutral heroism and complicated the stereotype equating women with peace and passive victimhood by documenting women's active violence as well as their suffering.

Jasmina Husanović, a Bosnian feminist scholar-activist at the University of Tuzla, develops what she terms a "politics of witnessing and hope," most fully set out in her 2009 *Feminist Theory* article "The Politics of Gender, Witnessing, Postcoloniality and Trauma: Bosnian Feminist Trajectories." She frames postwar Bosnian memory politics through a double critique: liberal transitional-justice instruments, trials and truth commissions, handle trauma with metaphorical "white gloves," treating it as a technical or legal problem, while nationalist memory politics claims ownership of victimhood for political ends; both, in her account, foreclose genuine reckoning. Against both, she looks for "openings in civil society," informal, state-independent spaces where art and cultural production let trauma be processed and witnessed rather than administered or instrumentalized. Her politics of witnessing centers "real bodies," those "defaced, erased and/or violently made non-relevant in operative biopolitical regimes," arguing that making such bodies visible, paired with material resources for those still struggling for survival and justice, is what gives the work transformative, hopeful potential rather than despairing closure. She casts this as "vernacular cosmopolitanism" rooted in Bosnia's own non-institutional public spaces, and models it through her self-identification as a "scholarly activist" working alongside, not above, civil-society organizers.

## Insights

- Takk's rehabilitation of his Waffen-SS service depends causally on the "prolonged rupture" frame: by treating 1940–1991 as one undifferentiated anti-Estonian occupation rather than three morally distinct episodes, it allows fighting in German uniform and later joining the Communist Party to read as the same continuous act of resistance rather than as contradictory choices.
- Takk's account of Communist Party membership as "internal resistance" mirrors, three decades on, his framing of Waffen-SS service as fighting for Estonia rather than for Germany: the throughline across incompatible regimes is not ideological consistency but a stable self-narrative device, "serving Estonia from inside," that outlives whichever system he is embedded in.
- Kõresaar's Takk case and Husanović's Bosnian analysis both diagnose nationalist "ownership" of trauma as a problem, but they respond in opposite registers: Kõresaar studies the phenomenon ethnographically from outside, analyzing how such narratives are built, while Husanović writes from inside the region as an activist proposing an alternative, civil-society witnessing, to the nationalist and transitional-justice options she sees crowding out Bosnian memory work.
- Stuklis's 2024 comparative study shows the prolonged-rupture/third-way/separate-war narrative pattern Kõresaar first identified in Takk recurs across other Estonian and Latvian Waffen-SS memoirists writing in the 1990s, indicating this is a structural convention of post-independence Baltic legionnaire life-writing rather than an idiosyncrasy specific to Takk.
- Aleksievich and Husanović share a structural move that Budrytė's synthesis treats as a common decolonizing strategy: both relocate authority over conflict memory away from state-level or nationalist "monumental" narratives and down to the level of individual bodies and voices, in explicit contrast to the top-down entrepreneurship exemplified by Takk.
- Aleksievich's "history of feelings" method carries a documented cost: while well attested for *War's Unwomanly Face* as an empowering, silence-respecting oral-history practice, the same creative-editing approach drew sustained criticism when applied to her later *Secondhand Time*, pointing to a durable tension in her genre between literary reconstruction of testimony and strict evidentiary fidelity.

## Strongest objection

Holocaust historians, notably Dovid Katz writing in *Dapim: Studies on the Holocaust*, argue that the "dual colonization"/"prolonged rupture" framing Kõresaar identifies as dominant in Estonia is the intellectual core of the broader, contested "Double Genocide" paradigm (codified in the 2008 Prague Declaration), which equates Nazi and Soviet crimes, challenges the Holocaust's historical uniqueness, and can serve to rehabilitate wartime collaborators and discredit survivors, a charge Takk's own self-legitimizing Waffen-SS narrative arguably illustrates even though he was unusually candid about the Holocaust relative to his peers. Separately, Aleksievich's "history of feelings" method has been criticized, per Sophie Pinkham writing in The New Republic (primarily regarding *Secondhand Time*), for creatively editing and embellishing testimony to the point that the work "falls short as both fact and art," a caution that extends to her documentary method more broadly.

## Summary

The public cloud computing market for IaaS and PaaS is highly concentrated, with three hyperscale providers—Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud—controlling 70-80% of global revenues and up to 80% in key markets such as the UK. Competition authorities across the EU, UK, Japan, South Korea, and the Netherlands have identified high barriers to switching driven by technical lock-in (proprietary APIs and data formats), contractual mechanisms (egress fees averaging $0.087-0.12 per gigabyte with markups in the thousands of percent over actual costs), and strategic pricing practices including large-scale cloud credit programs and service bundling. Regulatory findings point to concerns about vendor lock-in, limited interoperability, pricing opacity, and the dominance of established players limiting new entrants.

## Findings

### Part One: Market Structure and Competitive Dynamics

**Overview and Hyperscale Providers**

The global cloud infrastructure services market is dominated by three hyperscale providers: Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud Platform (GCP). These three companies control approximately 70-80% of the global cloud market by revenue. The US Federal Trade Commission has opened a formal investigation into Microsoft's cloud dominance, the European Commission has designated AWS and Azure as gatekeepers under the Digital Markets Act, and the UK Competition and Markets Authority concluded that "competition in the market is weak."

According to the Autorité de la concurrence (French competition authority), AWS and Microsoft captured 80% of the growth in spending on public cloud infrastructures and applications in France in 2021. In the United Kingdom, AWS and Microsoft Azure together controlled approximately 70-80% of the market in 2022, according to both Ofcom's market study and the UK Competition and Markets Authority. The European cloud market was dominated by the three American providers with a combined 70% share in 2024-2025, leaving European cloud providers such as SAP and Deutsche Telekom with only 15% of the European market combined. Japan's market showed Microsoft Azure with a particularly strong position at 49% of market share as of 2020, followed by AWS at 31% and Google Cloud at 20%, with customers increasingly purchasing through cloud resellers rather than directly from hyperscalers.

**Factors Leading to High Market Concentration**

Economies of scale play a central role in market concentration. AWS, Azure, and Google have each invested over $50 billion in global data centre infrastructure, creating capital barriers that prevent new entrants from competing at scale. The Autorité de la concurrence identified that "the likelihood of a new operator being able to gain market share rapidly appears limited" due to these structural advantages.

Network effects significantly amplify market concentration. Independent software vendors (ISVs) develop applications on the platforms controlling the largest user bases, creating dependency effects. Developers become familiar with specific cloud platforms during early career stages or through educational partnerships, establishing long-term vendor relationships. According to the OECD's May 2025 report on cloud competition, these ecosystem effects mean that "competing for the market is of more importance than competing in the market," allowing hyperscalers to offer broad service suites that lock customers into single platforms.

Integration with adjacent digital services extends market power. The bundling of cloud infrastructure with productivity software, AI services, and developer tools creates distribution advantages that smaller competitors cannot match. The scale of R&D investment by hyperscalers—cited by competition authorities as a differentiator—allows continuous expansion into new service categories that reinforce ecosystem lock-in.

### Part Two: Main Competition Issues and Anti-Competitive Practices

**1. Switching Barriers**

**Technical Barriers**

Cloud providers employ proprietary APIs and non-standard data formats that create technical dependencies. When organizations develop applications on specific cloud platforms, they write code against proprietary service APIs, triggers, and integration patterns. Migration requires significant rework and rewriting of integrations across application stacks. According to research cited in competition authority reports, 47% of enterprises identify data migration as a significant barrier when considering switching providers.

Cloud providers also employ vendor lock-in through integration of services that are not easily portable. The ACM (Dutch Authority for Consumers and Markets) identified that "technical incompatibilities, data portability limitations, and contractual restrictions make it difficult for customers to adopt multi-cloud strategies or change providers."

**Contractual and Financial Barriers**

Contract complexity creates switching friction. The Autorité de la concurrence noted "contractual imbalance between hyperscalers and their customers, resulting from the complexity of cloud offerings, which are often bundled, opaque and subject to frequent changes." Commitment discounts and long-term contract requirements reduce flexibility.

Data egress fees act as direct financial penalties for switching. According to Ofcom's UK market study, egress fees were identified as one of three key areas of regulatory concern. For a company storing 100 terabytes, a single migration costs approximately $9,000 in egress fees alone; moving 50 terabytes costs $3,500-7,000 in egress fees before accounting for migration tooling or service disruption costs.

**2. Pricing Issues**

**Egress Fees as Switching Penalties and Markup**

AWS charges $0.09 per gigabyte for data egress, Azure charges $0.087/GB, and Google Cloud charges $0.12/GB. For larger volumes above 50TB per month, Azure and AWS both charge $0.07/GB, then $0.05/GB above 150TB. The first 100GB per month is free on AWS and Azure; Google Cloud Premium Tier includes only the first 1GB free.

The markup over actual costs is substantial. According to analysis cited in competition authority reports and detailed in reports by cloud cost transparency researchers, hyperscaler egress fees represent "a markup of thousands of percent over wholesale internet transit costs." Cloud providers pay IP transit fees to backbone carriers and ISPs for every byte delivered to the public internet; hyperscalers set egress prices well above their transit costs, using the margin both as revenue and as a retention mechanism. The UK Competition and Markets Authority examined cloud provider cost-of-egress analysis and found the pricing structure difficult to justify on pure infrastructure grounds.

The EU Data Act, which entered force in January 2024 and has applied since September 2025, recognizes this lock-in mechanism; from January 12, 2027, providers serving EU customers will be prohibited from charging any switching fees, including data egress charges incurred during provider switches.

**Cloud Credit Programs**

Major cloud providers offer substantial subsidies to startups:
- AWS provides up to $100,000 in service credits, with AWS Generative AI Accelerator offering up to $1 million; AWS claims to have distributed more than $6 billion in credits to startups globally
- Microsoft Azure offers up to $150,000 in credits over two years through the Microsoft for Startups program, structured as $25,000 annually in year one, then $25,000 every six months thereafter
- Google Cloud offers up to $250,000 in credits through its Startups program for AI companies, with 20% rebate models for follow-on years

The Autorité de la concurrence flagged these programs as creating "lock-in risks." The programs are structured to establish relationships at critical early-stage moments when companies make architectural decisions. A seed-stage company receiving $250,000 in credits may later scale into an enterprise customer spending millions annually. By subsidizing infrastructure costs during growth phases, providers establish long-term relationships and switching becomes prohibitively expensive once applications are deeply integrated into proprietary services. Regulatory analysis notes these resemble anticompetitive "loyalty rebates," with cloud credits functioning as part of an overall "lock-in strategy."

**3. Exclusive Licensing and Bundling**

**Microsoft Bring Your Own License Policy Changes**

Microsoft modified its BYOL (Bring Your Own License) policy effective October 1, 2019. Historically, customers could purchase on-premises licenses for Windows Server, SQL Server, and other Microsoft enterprise software and deploy them on any cloud provider without cost penalty. The 2019 changes eliminated this freedom for licenses purchased without Software Assurance mobility rights.

Microsoft designated AWS, Google Cloud, and Alibaba as "listed providers" subject to restrictions. Customers running Microsoft software on these designated providers must now pay substantially more than if they ran the same software on Azure. This policy change raises the effective cost to competitors by increasing the total cost of ownership for enterprise customers migrating Microsoft-licensed workloads to non-Microsoft clouds. Google accused Microsoft in FTC proceedings of using "licensing restrictions that prevent customers, particularly existing on-premises enterprise clients, from choosing any other cloud provider at the time of migration into the cloud and ultimately locks those customers into its Azure ecosystem."

**Service Bundling and Anticompetitive Leverage**

The European Commission found Microsoft's bundling of Teams with Office 365 and Microsoft 365 commercial plans to be anticompetitive. The Commission concluded that bundling granted Teams a distribution advantage by not giving customers choice whether to acquire Teams when subscribing to SaaS productivity applications. Interoperability limitations between Teams' competitors (Slack, Zoom) and Microsoft's offerings further exacerbated competitive harm. The Commission noted that the way Microsoft bundled Teams "may have prevented Teams' alternatives such as Slack and Zoom from competing on a fair ground."

Microsoft agreed to make productivity suites available without Teams at reduced price following the Commission's probe, though the Commission preliminarily found even these concessions insufficient.

Similarly, Google bundles Google Cloud credits directly into its Google AI Pro and Ultra subscription tiers without extra cost, bundling them with Google Developer Program premium benefits. Azure bundles credits with GitHub Enterprise seats and Microsoft 365 licenses in its Founders Hub program. These bundling strategies integrate cloud infrastructure into broader service ecosystems, creating lock-in through multiple service dependencies.

## Insights

- **Market concentration stems from mutually reinforcing effects:** Capital requirements for data centre infrastructure create economies of scale that establish the Big Three, while network effects with developers and ISVs entrench their position, and bundling extends that dominance into adjacent markets, making escape prohibitively costly.

- **Switching costs are deliberately structural:** Egress fees alone create thousands-of-percent markups over actual costs; when combined with proprietary APIs, contractual complexity, long-term commitments, and cloud credits establishing early lock-in, switching costs become prohibitive rather than merely high.

- **Regulatory consensus has crystallized:** The EU (designating AWS and Azure as gatekeepers under the Digital Markets Act), UK CMA, French Autorité de la concurrence, Netherlands ACM, Japan JFTC, and US FTC have all independently identified the same competition issues—market concentration, lock-in through switching barriers and pricing, and exclusionary licensing practices—suggesting structural market failure rather than transient imbalance.

- **Policy intervention is shifting from investigation to enforcement:** The EU Data Act now prohibits egress fees in provider switches for EU customers starting January 2027; the FTC has escalated Microsoft investigation from inquiry to formal investigation; and the UK CMA is preparing strategic market status investigations, indicating competition authorities are moving toward remedial measures.

- **Lock-in operates through multiple channels simultaneously:** Technical (proprietary APIs), financial (egress fees, commitment discounts), contractual (complexity, opaque terms), and strategic (cloud credits, bundling) barriers interact to make switching not just expensive but organizationally complex, creating cumulative lock-in effects that exceed the impact of any single mechanism.

## Strongest objection

Some market observers argue that high market concentration does not necessarily indicate anticompetitive harm, noting that cloud costs have decreased over time and providers continue releasing new products and competing for customers; however, this argument does not address whether pricing reflects competitive outcomes or merely represents margin compression on a locked-in customer base, and does not account for the fact that traditional antitrust measures based on price effects are inadequate for evaluating markets where switching costs and lock-in mechanisms may suppress price competition entirely.
